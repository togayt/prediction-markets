Prediction markets attract a lot of questions about whether you can consistently make money. The honest answer is: yes, some people do: but it is harder than it looks, and most beginners lose money in the early stages.

Here is a realistic picture.

*Educational content only, not financial advice.*

## How money is made in prediction markets

There are a few legitimate paths to profit:

**Superior calibration.** If your probability estimates are consistently more accurate than the market's, you will profit over time. This is the cleanest edge and the hardest to build. It requires tracking your predictions, being honest about where you are right and wrong, and improving slowly.

**Information advantages.** You know something the market does not yet. A local voter who has ground-level information about turnout in a key precinct. A sports analyst who sees an injury the market has not priced in yet. These edges exist but are real-time and perishable.

**Market inefficiency on less-traded contracts.** Major events (US presidential elections, Fed decisions) are priced by thousands of informed traders. Niche events (a local referendum, a second-division sports league) may have thin participation and mispriced contracts. Finding these requires research.

**Arbitrage.** The same event sometimes has different prices on different platforms. If Kalshi shows 62¢ and another platform shows 55¢ for the same outcome, buying one and selling the other locks in a small profit. These gaps close quickly as arbitrageurs act on them.

## Why it is harder than it looks

**You are competing against informed people.** The traders on the other side of your trade include professional forecasters, quants, and people with deep domain knowledge. You are not playing against the casino; you are playing against the smartest people in the room.

**Costs add up.** Trading fees, spreads on entry and exit, and the bid-ask gap all reduce your returns. A 2% edge in probability can easily be eaten by a 4% round-trip cost in a thin market.

**Capital is locked up.** Your money sits in positions until resolution. A market on an election six months away ties up capital for six months, even if you are ultimately right.

**You need volume to validate an edge.** Luck dominates small samples. You might win 10 trades in a row by chance. You need hundreds of trades to know whether your edge is real. That takes time and capital.

**Resolution surprises happen.** Even correct predictions can lose due to resolution criteria you did not read carefully enough.

## What realistic returns look like

There is limited public data on average returns, but the pattern in similar markets (sports betting, financial markets) is consistent: a small percentage of traders are consistently profitable; the majority break even or lose.

Studies of prediction markets and sports betting consistently find that 70-80% of active traders are net losers. The winners tend to be:
- Highly calibrated across many events
- Disciplined about position sizes
- Ruthlessly honest about their actual edge

If you are starting out, assume you will lose money on net for the first year. The goal in that period is to lose small amounts while learning fast.

## Setting realistic expectations

A beginner with a modest budget should aim to:

1. **Learn the mechanics** without losing significant money. This means small position sizes on liquid markets.
2. **Build a track record.** Record every trade. After 50+ trades, look at whether your stated probability estimates are calibrated.
3. **Identify your actual area of expertise.** Where do you genuinely know more than the average market participant? That is where to focus.
4. **Scale up slowly.** Only increase position sizes after evidence of consistent positive expected value.

## Who does well

In practice, the people who profit consistently from prediction markets tend to share some traits:

- They have deep expertise in one or two specific domains (a macroeconomist trading Fed markets, a political scientist trading elections)
- They track everything meticulously
- They size positions conservatively and do not chase losses
- They treat it more like a part-time analytical job than gambling

If that describes you in your area of interest, prediction markets may be a good fit. If you are approaching it as pure entertainment or a quick way to make money, set a strict budget you are comfortable losing entirely.

## The bottom line

Making consistent money on prediction markets is possible but genuinely difficult. The honest picture is that most people starting out will lose money in their early trades. With discipline, honest self-assessment, and genuine expertise in specific topics, some traders build a durable edge.

Approach it like any other form of investing or speculative trading: understand the mechanics, start small, track everything, and be brutally honest about where you have real knowledge.
