Election markets are where most people first encounter prediction markets. Prices on who will win the presidency, a Senate seat, or a local ballot measure get quoted in news articles and compared to polls. Here is how they actually work and what you should know before you trade one.

*Educational content only, not financial advice.*

## Why election markets are different

Most financial instruments track something continuous: a stock price, an interest rate, a currency. Election markets are binary: a candidate either wins or loses. That makes them simpler in one way (you just pick a side) but harder in another (there is no partial credit for being close).

A contract on "Candidate A wins the presidential election" will eventually settle at exactly $1 or exactly $0. If you buy at 55¢ and the candidate wins, you earn 45¢ per contract. If the candidate loses, you lose 55¢ per contract.

## What drives election market prices

Several inputs push prices around:

**Polls.** New polling data is the most frequent mover. A swing of a few points in a key state can shift prices noticeably, especially close to election day.

**Prediction model outputs.** Sites like FiveThirtyEight (now rebuilt), The Economist, and others publish probabilistic models. Traders compare these to market prices and trade on differences.

**News and events.** Major gaffes, endorsements, health events, or scandals move markets quickly: sometimes before polls reflect them.

**Money flows.** Large buy orders can temporarily push prices around in thinner markets. Watch for prices that look out of line with the broader picture.

**Late-closing markets.** Some markets stay open until very close to election day. Prices can move sharply in the final days as early-vote data and turnout models emerge.

## How to approach an election market as a beginner

**Start with high-profile races.** Presidential and major Senate elections attract the most traders, meaning tighter spreads and prices that reflect more collective information.

**Compare prices across platforms.** Kalshi and Polymarket (where accessible) often show slightly different prices on the same race. Large differences suggest one market has new information or is mispriced.

**Use polling averages, not single polls.** A single poll is noisy. Aggregators like RealClearPolitics or 538-successors smooth out the noise. If a market price diverges significantly from a credible aggregate, that is worth investigating.

**Watch the electoral map, not just national numbers.** In the US presidential race, national polls are less useful than state-level numbers in key swing states. A market on the national popular vote is a different bet than one on the Electoral College outcome.

## Timing and settlement

Election markets have unusual timing risks:

**Results night vs. official certification.** The market's resolution criteria might specify when it settles. Some settle on the called winner on election night; others wait for certification, which can be weeks later. Read this carefully: a disputed election with a clear night-of winner but delayed certification could leave you in a position for a long time.

**Recounts and legal challenges.** Markets typically define what happens in contested outcomes. Check whether the resolution source is the official canvass, news network calls, or something else.

**Holding capital.** Your money is tied up until resolution. On long-cycle events (primaries followed by generals), capital can be locked up for months.

## The common mistakes in election markets

**Buying on your preferred outcome.** Wanting a candidate to win does not make them more likely to win. This is the single most common and expensive mistake in political trading.

**Ignoring structural factors.** Incumbency advantages, economic conditions, and historical base rates are powerful priors. Do not discount them because of a dramatic recent development.

**Over-updating on a single poll.** Individual polls have large margins of error. A single poll showing a 10-point shift is almost certainly noise. Wait for confirmation.

**Trading too close to resolution without new information.** Prices on heavily-traded election markets near resolution are usually efficient. If you have no new information, you are likely just taking the other side of someone who does.

## A note on 2026 and beyond

Election markets will be active throughout the 2026 US midterm cycle: Senate seats, House control, state governorships, and ballot measures. Check what is available on Kalshi and Polymarket (where accessible) as the cycle heats up.

Markets on international elections: UK, France, Germany, Australia, and others: are generally available on Polymarket for non-US traders.

*Always confirm what markets are available in your region and read resolution criteria before trading. This is not a recommendation to trade any specific political outcome.*
