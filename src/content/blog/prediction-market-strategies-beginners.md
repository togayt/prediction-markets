Prediction markets reward clear thinking, patience, and discipline more than speed or insider knowledge. These five strategies are a practical starting point for anyone placing their first trades.

*Educational content only, not financial advice. Prediction markets involve risk: only use money you can afford to lose.*

## 1. Start with high-liquidity markets

Before worrying about finding an edge, learn how trading actually works by using markets where the mechanics run smoothly.

High-liquidity markets: major elections, Fed rate decisions, big sports championships: have tight spreads, fast execution, and quick feedback. When your Yes contract settles at $1 or $0, you learn the whole cycle (entry, position monitoring, resolution) without being penalised by wide spreads or stale prices.

Thin markets are fine later, but they hide problems from beginners. Start where there is real volume.

## 2. Compare the price to your actual estimate

The core skill in prediction markets is forming an independent probability estimate and comparing it to the current price.

Do not just look at a price and feel it seems high or low. Write down your own estimate first:

1. Look at the question carefully
2. Think about the evidence (polls, data, history, base rates)
3. Write down a number: "I think this has a 55% chance of happening"
4. Compare to the market price

If your estimate is 55% and the market says 40%, you have a potential edge buying Yes. If your estimate matches the market, there is no edge and you should probably not trade.

This process also catches overconfidence. Most beginners who do this exercise realise they have no informed view on most markets: and that is useful to know before you spend money.

## 3. Keep positions small and diversify

No single prediction is certain. A well-reasoned 70% chance still loses 30% of the time.

Keep any individual position to a small fraction of what you are willing to put into prediction markets overall. A useful starting rule: no single trade should risk more than 5-10% of your total prediction market budget.

This lets you survive the inevitable losing trades without being wiped out before you can learn and improve.

## 4. Understand the resolution rules before you buy

This is the most overlooked part of prediction market trading. Every market has written resolution criteria that define exactly when and how it settles. These matter enormously.

Common surprises:
- A sports market that resolves on the winner after regulation only, excluding overtime
- A political market that uses an "official certification" date that is months after election night
- A market on an economic figure that specifies a particular release date and revision policy

Read the resolution criteria before you enter. Winning the underlying event but losing your trade because of a technicality is entirely avoidable.

## 5. Track your trades and review them

Most beginner traders rely on memory, which is unreliable and biased toward remembering wins.

Keep a simple log:
- Market name
- Your entry price
- Your stated estimate at entry
- Outcome
- Actual resolution probability (what really happened)

After 20-30 trades, patterns emerge. Were your 60% estimates actually winning about 60% of the time? Were you systematically overconfident on one type of event? Did you consistently buy at worse prices than you intended?

This feedback loop is what turns a casual trader into someone who improves. Without it, you are just guessing repeatedly.

## What to avoid

A few patterns that consistently cost beginners money:

**Chasing recent events.** Buying Yes on something just because it dominated the news this week. Markets usually price in major news quickly.

**Confusing wanting something to happen with it being likely.** Your preferred election outcome is not more probable because you prefer it.

**Trading thin markets without a genuine edge.** Wide spreads mean you are paying a lot just to enter. In a thin market, your edge needs to be very large to cover that cost.

**Holding through resolution out of stubbornness.** If new information arrives that changes the picture, it is fine to exit before resolution even at a small loss.

## The real edge is calibration

The traders who do well over time are not the ones who find obscure information. They are the ones whose probability estimates are consistently accurate: when they say 70%, things happen roughly 70% of the time.

Calibration takes practice, honest record-keeping, and the willingness to be wrong. It is a skill you build slowly, not something you start with.
